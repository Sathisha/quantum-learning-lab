Trigonometry refresher

Sines and cosines show up in every part of quantum computing: the amplitudes cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2) on the Bloch sphere, the rotation matrices, the probabilities cos², and Euler's eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ. This page rebuilds the trigonometry you need: the unit circle, radians, the special values, and the four identities the course keeps using.

Back to basics: the right triangle

School trigonometry starts with a right-angled triangle. For an angle θ in it:

SOH-CAH-TOA

sin θ = opposite / hypotenuse

cos θ = adjacent / hypotenuse

tan θ = opposite / adjacent = sin θ / cos θ

Make the hypotenuse length 1 and the fractions disappear: the sides are simply cos θ and sin θ. Pythagoras (a² + b² = c²) then gives the most used identity in the course:

cos²θ + sin²θ = 1

The unit circle: trigonometry for every angle

A triangle only allows angles between 0° and 90°. The unit circle (radius 1, centre at the origin) removes that limit: walk anticlockwise from the point (1, 0) through an angle θ. The point you land on defines cos θ and sin θ:

point at angle θ = (cos θ, sin θ)

Say: "cos theta is how far right, sin theta is how far up."

Three facts to read straight off the circle
  • cos θ and sin θ are always between −1 and 1.
  • cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 for every θ: the point is always at distance 1 from the centre.
  • Going once round (θ → θ + 2π) brings you back: cos and sin repeat every 2π.

Radians: the unit the maths uses

A radian measures an angle by the arc length it cuts out of the unit circle. The whole circumference is 2π, so a full turn is 2π radians.

360° = 2π rad · 180° = π rad
degrees → radians: multiply by π/180 90° × π/180 = π/2 radians → degrees: multiply by 180/π π/4 × 180/π = 45° Handy anchors: π/2 = quarter turn, π = half turn, 2π = full turn.
Calculators and NumPy use radians. np.cos(90) is the cosine of 90 radians (≈ −0.448), not of 90°. Write np.cos(np.pi/2) or np.cos(np.deg2rad(90)).

Symmetries: get any angle from a small one

Each rule is a reflection or a turn of the unit circle, so you can always check it by picturing the point.

cos(−θ) = cos θ, sin(−θ) = −sin θMirror in the x-axis. Cos is even, sin is odd. This is why the complex conjugate of eiθ is e−iθ.

The addition formulas

These two formulas are the engine behind the double-angle and half-angle rules and the rotation matrix. On the eiθ page you will see them fall out of eia·eib = ei(a+b) in two lines, so if you only remember that, you can rebuild them.

cos(a + b) = cos a cos b − sin a sin b
sin(a + b) = sin a cos b + cos a sin b
The classic mistake. sin(a + b) is not sin a + sin b. Check with a = b = 45°: sin 90° = 1, but sin 45° + sin 45° ≈ 1.414.

Set b = a: the double-angle formulas

cos 2a = cos²a − sin²a = 2cos²a − 1 (use sin²a = 1 − cos²a) = 1 − 2sin²a (use cos²a = 1 − sin²a) sin 2a = 2 sin a cos a

The half-angle formulas: the Bloch sphere's favourite

Solve the two versions of cos 2a for cos²a and sin²a, then write θ for 2a:

cos²(θ/2) = (1 + cos θ)/2 · sin²(θ/2) = (1 − cos θ)/2

Say: "cos squared of half theta is one plus cos theta, over two."

Here is why the course cares. A qubit at Bloch angle θ is cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩, so

P(0) = cos²(θ/2) = (1 + cos θ)/2 = (1 + z)/2 P(1) = sin²(θ/2) = (1 − cos θ)/2 = (1 − z)/2 z = cos θ is the height of the point on the sphere. The probability is a straight-line function of height.

Worked example, every step shown

A qubit sits at θ = 60° (π/3) from the north pole. What is P(0)?

Route 1, directly: θ/2 = 30°, cos 30° = √3/2, P(0) = (√3/2)² = 3/4 Route 2, half-angle: cos 60° = 1/2, P(0) = (1 + 1/2)/2 = (3/2)/2 = 3/4 ✓ P(1) = 1 − 3/4 = 1/4 The state is (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)|1⟩. Squaring the amplitudes gives 3/4 and 1/4.

The rotation matrix, derived from the addition formulas

Take the point at angle a, (cos a, sin a), and turn it by an extra angle b. It lands at angle a + b:

new x = cos(a+b) = cos b·cos a − sin b·sin a new y = sin(a+b) = sin b·cos a + cos b·sin a Each new coordinate is a fixed mix of the old ones (cos a, sin a), with weights: ┌ new x ┐ ┌ cos b −sin b ┐ ┌ cos a ┐ └ new y ┘ = └ sin b cos b ┘ └ sin a ┘

So "rotate by b" is multiplication by the matrix Rb = [[cos b, −sin b], [sin b, cos b]]. This matrix appears on the linear maps page. Its eigenvalues are e±ib (eigenvalues page), which is where trigonometry, complex numbers and eigenvalues meet.

Rotating twice by b is rotating once by 2b: RbRb = R2b. Multiply it out and you get the double-angle formulas again.

Common mistakes

  • Degrees in a radians function. NumPy, Python's math and most of the maths assume radians.
  • sin²θ vs sin(θ²). sin²θ means (sin θ)².
  • sin⁻¹ x vs 1/sin x. sin⁻¹ is the inverse function (arcsin). 1/sin x is written csc x, or just 1/sin x.
  • Splitting sums. sin(a + b) ≠ sin a + sin b, and cos(2a) ≠ 2 cos a.
  • Forgetting the half on the Bloch sphere. The amplitude uses θ/2, not θ. |1⟩ is at θ = π (south pole), and cos(π/2) = 0 ✓.

Practice

  1. Convert 135° to radians, and 5π/6 to degrees.
  2. Use the addition formula to find sin 75° exactly (75 = 45 + 30).
  3. A qubit sits on the equator (θ = 90°). Use the half-angle formula to find P(0).
  4. Use cos(π − θ) = −cos θ to find cos 150°.
Show worked solutions
1. 135 × π/180 = 3π/4. 5π/6 × 180/π = 150°. 2. sin 75° = sin 45° cos 30° + cos 45° sin 30° = (√2/2)(√3/2) + (√2/2)(1/2) = √2(√3 + 1)/4 = (√6 + √2)/4 ≈ 0.9659 3. P(0) = (1 + cos 90°)/2 = (1 + 0)/2 = 1/2 (every equator state is 50/50) 4. cos 150° = cos(180° − 30°) = −cos 30° = −√3/2 ≈ −0.866

Check it in NumPy

import numpy as np
print(np.cos(np.pi/3), np.sin(np.pi/6))        # 0.5 0.5
print(np.sin(np.deg2rad(75)), (np.sqrt(6)+np.sqrt(2))/4)   # 0.9659 twice
th = np.pi/3                                     # Bloch angle 60°
print(np.cos(th/2)**2, (1 + np.cos(th))/2)       # 0.75 0.75
R = lambda b: np.array([[np.cos(b), -np.sin(b)], [np.sin(b), np.cos(b)]])
print(np.allclose(R(0.3) @ R(0.3), R(0.6)))      # True: two turns = one double turn
