Concept 13 · Part C · One qubit

One qubit, the whole picture

The first three weeks gave us one qubit in four languages: a column vector in ℂ², a ket, an arrow on the Bloch sphere, and a gate as a turn of that sphere. This page joins them up. Orthonormal bases become opposite points, measurements become diameters, gates become rotations, a gate's eigenvectors become its axis, and its eigenvalues become the angle it turns through. It is where the trigonometry, eiθ, inner product and eigenvalue pages all meet.

1 · A state is a point on the sphere

A qubit α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ has two complex numbers, which is four real numbers. Normalisation (|α|² + |β|² = 1) removes one, and the invisible global phase (eiθ page) removes another. That leaves two angles:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφ sin(θ/2)|1⟩

Read the angles as a point on a sphere, the Bloch vector:

n = (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ)
From amplitudes directly (handy in code), with |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩: x = 2·Re(ᾱβ) y = 2·Im(ᾱβ) z = |α|² − |β|²
StateColumn(θ, φ)Bloch point
|0⟩(1, 0)(0, –)north pole (0, 0, 1)
|1⟩(0, 1)(π, –)south pole (0, 0, −1)
|+⟩(1, 1)/√2(π/2, 0)+x (1, 0, 0)
|−⟩(1, −1)/√2(π/2, π)−x (−1, 0, 0)
|+i⟩(1, i)/√2(π/2, π/2)+y (0, 1, 0)
|−i⟩(1, −i)/√2(π/2, 3π/2)−y (0, −1, 0)

Latitude sets the odds and longitude sets the phase (Bloch sphere page): θ alone fixes P(0) = cos²(θ/2), and φ alone is the relative phase.

2 · Why θ/2? Opposite points are orthogonal vectors

|0⟩ and |1⟩ are orthogonal: 90° apart as vectors in ℂ². On the sphere they are the poles, 180° apart. Halving the angle is what turns "opposite on the sphere" into "orthogonal in ℂ²". Here is the check for every point. The point opposite (θ, φ) is (π − θ, φ + π):

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2)|1⟩ |ψ⊥⟩ = cos((π−θ)/2)|0⟩ + e^(i(φ+π)) sin((π−θ)/2)|1⟩ = sin(θ/2)|0⟩ − e^(iφ) cos(θ/2)|1⟩ cos(π/2 − x) = sin x, e^(iπ) = −1 ⟨ψ|ψ⊥⟩ = cos(θ/2)·sin(θ/2) + conj(e^(iφ) sin(θ/2))·(−e^(iφ) cos(θ/2)) = cos(θ/2) sin(θ/2) − e^(−iφ)e^(iφ) sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2) = 0 ✓ e^(−iφ)e^(iφ) = 1
The general overlap rule. If the Bloch vectors of |ψ⟩ and |χ⟩ are an angle γ apart, then |⟨χ|ψ⟩|² = cos²(γ/2) = (1 + n·m)/2. The same point gives 1, a quarter turn apart (|0⟩ and |+⟩, or |+⟩ and |+i⟩) gives 1/2, and opposite points give 0. The second form is the half-angle formula.

3 · An orthonormal basis is a diameter, and a diameter is a measurement

An orthonormal basis of ℂ² is two unit vectors with ⟨b₁|b₂⟩ = 0 (orthonormal bases). By section 2, that is two opposite points, which is one diameter of the sphere. Every diameter is a possible measurement, and the three axes are the three standard ones:

BasisKetsLinear-algebra namesDiameter
Z (computational)|0⟩, |1⟩e₁, e₂z-axis
X (Hadamard)|+⟩, |−⟩h₊, h₋x-axis
Y (circular)|+i⟩, |−i⟩c₊, c₋y-axis

The Born rule, in any basis

P(x) = |⟨x|ψ⟩|² = ⟨ψ|Px|ψ⟩, Px = |x⟩⟨x|

After the measurement the state is |x⟩. Geometrically, the outcome probability is the "shadow" of the arrow on that diameter: P(+n end) = (1 + n·m)/2. Each basis sees only one coordinate of the arrow:

StateZ basis P(0), P(1)X basis P(+), P(−)Y basis P(+i), P(−i)
|0⟩1, 0½, ½½, ½
|+⟩½, ½1, 0½, ½
|+i⟩½, ½½, ½1, 0

So |+⟩ and |+i⟩ look identical in the Z basis but completely different in the X and Y bases. This is "relative phase is observable", stated precisely. In a circuit the hardware only measures Z, so to measure in X you apply H first. That is exactly what the final H in Deutsch's algorithm does.

Completeness: the identity, written in any basis. |0⟩⟨0| + |1⟩⟨1| = I = |+⟩⟨+| + |−⟩⟨−|. Apply either side to |ψ⟩ to see it: you get ⟨0|ψ⟩|0⟩ + ⟨1|ψ⟩|1⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. You can insert I anywhere to switch basis. Summing the projectors is also why the probabilities add to 1.

4 · A gate is a rotation of the sphere

A gate is unitary: U†U = I. Unitaries keep every inner product, ⟨Uφ|Uψ⟩ = ⟨φ|U†U|ψ⟩ = ⟨φ|ψ⟩, so they keep every overlap. By the overlap rule, they keep every angle between Bloch arrows. A map of the sphere that keeps all angles and can be undone is a rigid rotation. That is why every single-qubit gate "turns the ball".

Writing a gate from where it sends the basis

If a gate sends |0⟩ → |u₀⟩ and |1⟩ → |u₁⟩, then

U = |u₀⟩⟨0| + |u₁⟩⟨1|

Read it as instructions: "take the |0⟩-part, output u₀; take the |1⟩-part, output u₁". It is the same rule as "column j is the image of ej" (outer products):

GateIn wordsDirac form
Xswap 0 ↔ 1|1⟩⟨0| + |0⟩⟨1|
Zsign on |1⟩|0⟩⟨0| − |1⟩⟨1|
H0 → +, 1 → −|+⟩⟨0| + |−⟩⟨1|
Algebra in one line. H is Hermitian (H† = H), so also H = |0⟩⟨+| + |1⟩⟨−|. Then: H|+⟩ = |0⟩⟨+|+⟩ + |1⟩⟨−|+⟩ = |0⟩·1 + |1⟩·0 = |0⟩ "H twice gives back |0⟩" is just the single fact ⟨−|+⟩ = 0.

5 · Eigenvectors are the axis; eigenvalues give the angle

A rotation leaves exactly two points where they are: the two ends of its axis. In vector language, a state that a gate "leaves alone" is one it only multiplies by a number, U|v⟩ = λ|v⟩, which is an eigenvector (eigenvalues page). The extra λ is a pure phase (|λ| = 1 for a unitary), so it is a global phase and the point really does not move.

Spectral form. A gate's two eigenvectors are orthogonal (opposite ends of the axis), so they form a basis, and the gate is U = λ₁|v₁⟩⟨v₁| + λ₂|v₂⟩⟨v₂|. A gate is completely fixed by its eigenvectors and its eigenvalues.

Now put in any state, written in that basis:

|ψ⟩ = a|v₁⟩ + b|v₂⟩ U|ψ⟩ = λ₁a|v₁⟩ + λ₂b|v₂⟩ = λ₁ ( a|v₁⟩ + (λ₂/λ₁) b|v₂⟩ ) λ₁ out front is a global phase. λ₂/λ₁ = e^(iγ) is a relative phase, and relative phase is longitude measured round the v₁ pole. So U turns the sphere by γ = arg(λ₂/λ₁) about the axis through v₁.
GateEigenvectors (λ)AxisTurn γ
Z|0⟩ (1), |1⟩ (−1)zπ, half turn
X|+⟩ (1), |−⟩ (−1)xπ
Y|+i⟩ (1), |−i⟩ (−1)yπ
S|0⟩ (1), |1⟩ (i)zπ/2, quarter turn
T|0⟩ (1), |1⟩ (eiπ/4)zπ/4, eighth turn
H(cos π/8, sin π/8) (1), (−sin π/8, cos π/8) (−1)halfway between x and zπ
The Hadamard row is where everything meets. In the real plane, H is a mirror across the line at π/8 = 22.5°. That line is its +1 eigenvector. As a qubit, (cos π/8, sin π/8) has θ/2 = π/8, so θ = π/4 on the sphere, and its Bloch axis is (x + z)/√2. The angle doubles on the way from ℂ² to the sphere, exactly as in section 2. A half turn about that diagonal swaps the x and z axes, which is why H turns |0⟩ into |+⟩.

Only the ratio of the eigenvalues matters. Multiply a gate by a global phase and both eigenvalues turn together, so the rotation is unchanged. diag(e−iπ/4, eiπ/4) and S = diag(1, i) are the same quarter turn.

6 · Conjugation moves the axis: HZH = X, proved

Sandwich a gate M between U and U†. Using the spectral form and (U|v⟩)† = ⟨v|U†:

U (Σ λk|vk⟩⟨vk|) U† = Σ λk |Uvk⟩⟨Uvk|

The eigenvalues stay; the eigenvectors are carried along by U. On the sphere: the same turn, about an axis that U has moved.

HZH = H ( |0⟩⟨0| − |1⟩⟨1| ) H = (H|0⟩)(⟨0|H) − (H|1⟩)(⟨1|H) = |+⟩⟨+| − |−⟩⟨−| H|0⟩ = |+⟩ and ⟨0|H = (H|0⟩)† = ⟨+|, since H† = H = X X's spectral form: eigenvectors |±⟩, eigenvalues ±1

H swaps the z and x axes, so it turns a z-turn into an x-turn. The same argument gives HXH = Z. Circuit meaning: H–Z–H on a wire acts as X, and an H on each side of a CNOT's target turns it into a controlled-Z. The gates page shows the same identity with matrices.

One trace that uses every idea

Start at |0⟩, apply H, then S, then measure in each of the three bases.

|0⟩ north pole H|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 = |+⟩ half turn about (x+z)/√2: north pole → +x S|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2 = |+i⟩ quarter turn about z: +x → +y (eigenvalues 1, i → γ = π/2) Z basis: P(0) = |⟨0|+i⟩|² = |1/√2|² = 1/2 X basis: ⟨+|+i⟩ = ½(1·1 + 1·i) = (1 + i)/2, |·|² = 2/4 = 1/2 Y basis: ⟨+i|+i⟩ = ½(1·1 + conj(i)·i) = ½(1 + 1) = 1 certain

The arrow points along +y. The Z and X bases each see a zero coordinate, so they give 50/50; the Y basis sees the whole arrow, so the outcome is certain.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up 90° and 180°. Orthogonal vectors (90° in ℂ²) are opposite points (180° on the sphere).
  • Reading an eigenvalue as the angle. The turn is the phase difference of the two eigenvalues. Z has eigenvalues 1 and −1 and turns by π.
  • Thinking the eigenvectors move. They only pick up a phase, which is invisible. Every other state moves.
  • Forgetting the conjugate in ⟨x|ψ⟩ when x has an i in it (the Y basis).
  • Treating "measure in X" as a different machine. It is "apply H, then measure in Z".

Practice

  1. What axis does T turn about, and by how much? Where does T send |+⟩ (give θ and φ)?
  2. A state sits 60° from the north pole. What is |⟨0|ψ⟩|²? Use the overlap rule.
  3. What does S† do on the sphere? Compute S†|+i⟩.
  4. Use conjugation to predict S X S†, then check it with matrices.

Check it in NumPy

import numpy as np
def bloch(psi):
    a, b = psi
    return np.real([2*np.conj(a)*b, -2j*np.conj(a)*b, abs(a)**2 - abs(b)**2]).round(6)
H = np.array([[1, 1], [1, -1]])/np.sqrt(2)
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(H)
print(w, bloch(V[:, 1]))                    # [-1. 1.]  [0.7071 0 0.7071]: axis (x+z)/√2
print(V[:, 1], np.cos(np.pi/8), np.sin(np.pi/8))   # the +1 eigenvector is at 22.5° (up to sign)
S = np.diag([1, 1j]); plus = np.array([1, 1])/np.sqrt(2)
print(bloch(S @ plus))                      # [0 1 0]: |+⟩ → |+i⟩
Z, X = np.diag([1, -1]), np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]])
print(np.allclose(H @ Z @ H, X), np.allclose(S @ X @ S.conj().T, [[0, -1j], [1j, 0]]))   # True True
