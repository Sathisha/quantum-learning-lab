Qubits and Dirac notation

New spelling for the vectors you met on the inner products concept. Nothing here is new maths: a qubit's state is a length‑1 arrow, and the brackets are just a compact way to write it.

Back to basics

Three facts from earlier days carry all of today's arithmetic:

  • Root two squared is two. √2 · √2 = 2, so (1/√2)² = 1/2. This is why 1/√2 shows up everywhere in quantum computing: two halves make one.
  • Conjugate flips the sign of i. (3 + 4i)* = 3 − 4i. A real number is its own conjugate: (0.6)* = 0.6.
  • Inner product (the inner products concept): conjugate the first vector, multiply matching entries, add.

Tiny worked example: square 1/√2.

(1/√2)² = (1/√2) · (1/√2)
= (1 · 1) / (√2 · √2)
= 1 / 2
= 0.5
Check: 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071, and 0.7071 × 0.7071 ≈ 0.49999 ≈ 0.5 ✓

The idea

A qubit's state is a column of two numbers, (α, β), whose squared sizes add up to 1. Physicists write that column as a ket, |ψ⟩, and name the two basis columns |0⟩ and |1⟩, so any state reads α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. A superposition just means both α and β are non‑zero. Flip a ket into a conjugated row and you get a bra, ⟨ψ|; put a bra in front of a ket and the two click into ⟨φ|ψ⟩, the inner products concept's inner product.

Diagrams

Key definition

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩
with
|α|² + |β|² = 1
Read it aloud: "ket psi equals alpha times ket zero plus beta times ket one, where mod alpha squared plus mod beta squared equals one."
In plain words: a qubit is a mix of 0 and 1; square each amplitude's size to get the chance of measuring that outcome, and the chances must total 1. Equivalently, ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1 ("bra psi ket psi equals one").

Worked example

Is |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ + (4i/5)|1⟩ a valid qubit state? If so, what are the measurement probabilities?

Step 1 · write the ket as a column:   |ψ⟩ = (3/5, 4i/5)
Step 2 · make the bra (conjugate each entry):
  (3/5)* = 3/5   (real, unchanged)
  (4i/5)* = −4i/5   (sign of i flips)
  ⟨ψ| = (3/5, −4i/5)
Step 3 · ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = (3/5)·(3/5) + (−4i/5)·(4i/5)
Step 4 · first product: (3/5)·(3/5) = (3·3)/(5·5) = 9/25
Step 5 · second product: (−4i/5)·(4i/5) = (−4·4)·(i·i)/(5·5)
  = (−16)·(i²)/25
  = (−16)·(−1)/25   (because i² = −1)
  = 16/25
Step 6 · add: 9/25 + 16/25 = 25/25 = 1  → valid state ✓
Step 7 · probabilities: P(0) = |3/5|² = 9/25 = 0.36
  P(1) = |4i/5|² = (4/5)² = 16/25 = 0.64
Check: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1.00 ✓  Same 3‑4‑5 triangle as Pythagoras: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5².

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to conjugate the bra. With the example above, skipping it gives 9/25 + (4i/5)·(4i/5) = 9/25 + 16i²/25 = 9/25 − 16/25 = −7/25. A negative "length squared" is the giveaway that something went wrong.
  • Reading |0⟩ as the zero vector. |0⟩ is the column (1, 0): a perfectly good arrow of length 1. The label "0" names the outcome, not the size.
  • Treating amplitudes as probabilities. |+⟩ has amplitudes 0.7071 each, but the probabilities are 0.7071² = 0.5 each. Always square the size first.

Check question

Write |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 as a column vector and check it is normalised.

Show answer
Step 1 · substitute the columns: |0⟩ = (1, 0), |1⟩ = (0, 1)
Step 2 · add them: (1, 0) + (0, 1) = (1 + 0, 0 + 1) = (1, 1)
Step 3 · divide each entry by √2: |+⟩ = (1/√2, 1/√2) ≈ (0.7071, 0.7071)
Step 4 · bra: entries are real, so ⟨+| = (1/√2, 1/√2) (nothing changes)
Step 5 · ⟨+|+⟩ = (1/√2)·(1/√2) + (1/√2)·(1/√2)
Step 6 · each product: (1·1)/(√2·√2) = 1/2
Step 7 · add: 1/2 + 1/2 = 2/2 = 1
⟨+|+⟩ = 1, so |+⟩ is normalised ✓ Meaning: measuring |+⟩ gives 0 with probability 1/2 and 1 with probability 1/2.

Check it in NumPy

import numpy as np
plus = np.array([1, 1]) / np.sqrt(2)   # |+> as a column
print(plus)                            # [0.7071 0.7071]
print(np.vdot(plus, plus))             # 1.0 -> normalised (vdot conjugates the first vector)

You may see 0.9999999999999998 instead of 1.0. That is rounding inside the computer, not a mistake.

A ket is just a name tag

Whatever is written inside | ⟩ is a label, not a number. |0⟩, |+⟩, |ψ⟩ are all names for column vectors with two complex entries. Dirac invented the bracket so that long multi-qubit expressions stay readable.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = column (α, β) |+⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩ + |1⟩) = column (1/√2, 1/√2) |−⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩ − |1⟩) = column (1/√2, −1/√2) Read aloud: "ket psi equals alpha ket zero plus beta ket one".

Class uses kets from now on. Bras ⟨·|, inner products in bra-ket form and outer products are formalised in the third lecture (Sat 31 Oct). This page already covers them, so you are ahead.

The three-filter puzzle

A polarising filter lets through light whose "wobble direction" lines up with it. Two filters at 0° and 90° (crossed) block all light. Now slide a third filter at 45° between them, and light gets through again. Adding a filter lets more light pass. How?

After the 0° filter the photon is |0⟩ (vertical). 45° filter measures in {|+⟩, |−⟩}. Pass chance = |⟨+|0⟩|² ⟨+|0⟩ = (1/√2)(1) + (1/√2)(0) = 1/√2 → |1/√2|² = 1/2 A photon that passed is now |+⟩. The "vertical" record is gone. 90° filter: pass chance = |⟨1|+⟩|² = |1/√2|² = 1/2 Total: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 25% ✓ Without the middle filter: |⟨1|0⟩|² = 0² = 0. Nothing passes.

Shot one photon at a time, each one passes or is absorbed at random. Only the probability is fixed: the counter in the class animation settles at ¼. The lecturer's homework: re-derive this 25% yourself with pen and paper.

Polarisation is a qubit (not an analogy)

Physical stateNotation
Vertical, |V⟩|0⟩
Horizontal, |H⟩|1⟩
45° diagonal|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2

Other real qubits: electron spin up/down, energy levels of a superconducting circuit, internal states of a trapped ion, defects ("NV centres") in diamond.

Caveat: angles double. 0° and 90° light are perpendicular, so as qubit states they are orthogonal: opposite poles of the Bloch sphere, 180° apart. A 45° filter is halfway in the lab but lands on the equator, 90° from each pole.

One line to remember: a polariser is a measurement, and Malus's law (light passing = cos² of the angle between filters) is the Born rule. cos²(45°) = (1/√2)² = 1/2.

From class, Week 2 · slides 6–8

Bit vs qubit, and two pictures of superposition

Classical bit

Exactly two isolated states, 0 and 1. Operations are fixed maps {0,1} → {0,1}, such as NOT. Everything a classical computer does is sequences of these.

Qubit

A unit vector in ℂ²: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with |α|² + |β|² = 1. Infinitely many states, but one measurement still returns exactly one bit.

  • The spinning coin: while it spins it is neither heads nor tails; landing forces one answer.
  • The compass needle: it can point in any direction, not just north or south. Measuring asks "north or south?" and the needle's direction sets the odds.
Superposition is not "being in two places at once". It is one definite, specific state, just not a classical one.

The Born rule and what measuring does

For |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩:P(0) = |α|², P(1) = |β|²

Read aloud: "the probability of outcome zero is mod alpha squared". Linked to the inner product: P(0) = |⟨0|ψ⟩|² = |α|².

Worked example: measure |+⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩ Step 1 α = 1/√2, β = 1/√2 Step 2 P(0) = |1/√2|² = 1/2, P(1) = |1/√2|² = 1/2 Step 3 50% outcome 0, 50% outcome 1 Step 4 after outcome 0 the state has collapsed to |0⟩

The shadow analogy. Measuring is like a 3-D object casting a 2-D shadow: a continuous state is forced onto a one-bit answer.

  • The outcome is random, and the odds are set by the state (on the sphere: by the latitude).
  • Measurement destroys the superposition. Measure again and you get the same answer.
  • The state was not "secretly 0 or 1" before. The randomness is fundamental, not ignorance.
Trap: |+⟩ is not a coin flip. It gives 50/50 outcomes, but it is a definite quantum state. The difference shows up in interference: apply H and |+⟩ returns to |0⟩ every time. A coin you can't see would not.
