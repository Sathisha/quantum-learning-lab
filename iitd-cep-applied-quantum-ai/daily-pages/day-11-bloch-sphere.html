The Bloch sphere

Every single-qubit state is one point on a ball. Two angles say where the point is, the same way latitude and longitude say where a city is on a globe.

Back to basics: angles in radians, and cos² + sin² = 1

Maths measures angles in radians. A half turn is π radians, which is 180°. So to turn radians into degrees, multiply by 180 and divide by π. Useful landmarks: π/4 = 45°, π/2 = 90°, π = 180°, 2π = 360°.

The two facts you need from the unit circle (Day 1): cos 0 = 1 and sin 0 = 0; cos(π/2) = 0 and sin(π/2) = 1; cos(π/4) = sin(π/4) = 1/√2. And for every angle, cos² + sin² = 1 (Pythagoras on the unit circle).

Tiny example: turn π/4 into degrees, and check cos² + sin² = 1 there.

π/4 radians × (180° / π) = 180° / 4 = 45° cos(45°) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071 sin(45°) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071 cos² + sin² = (1/√2)² + (1/√2)² = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 ✓

The idea

A qubit is two complex numbers, but they must have total probability 1, and an overall phase on the whole state changes nothing you can measure. After those two rules, only two real numbers are left over. Two angles are exactly what you need to pick a point on the surface of a ball. So every single-qubit state is a point on a sphere: |0⟩ at the north pole, |1⟩ at the south pole, and equal superpositions such as |+⟩ and |−⟩ around the equator.

Diagrams

Key formula

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2) |0⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2) |1⟩

Read it aloud: "ket psi equals cos of theta over two, times ket zero, plus e to the i phi times sine of theta over two, times ket one." Here 0 ≤ θ ≤ π ("theta is between zero and pi") and 0 ≤ φ < 2π ("phi is from zero up to, but not including, two pi").

The probabilities come from Day 5: P(0) = cos²(θ/2) and P(1) = sin²(θ/2). The factor e^(iφ) has size 1, so φ never changes those probabilities. It only changes the phase, which matters once gates mix |0⟩ and |1⟩ together.

Worked example: θ = π/2, φ = π/2

Where is this point, and what is the state?

Step 1. Halve θ. θ/2 = (π/2) / 2 = π/4 (= 45°) Step 2. First amplitude. cos(π/4) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071 Step 3. Sine part of the second amplitude. sin(π/4) = 1/√2 ≈ 0.7071 Step 4. The phase factor (Euler, Day 1). e^(iπ/2) = cos(π/2) + i sin(π/2) = 0 + i × 1 = i Step 5. Put it together. |ψ⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + i × (1/√2)|1⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2 Step 6. Where is it? θ = π/2 → on the equator. φ = π/2 → a quarter turn from +x → on the +y axis. Check: probabilities add to 1. P(0) = |1/√2|² = 1/2 P(1) = |i/√2|² = (i/√2) × (−i/√2) = −i² / 2 = −(−1) / 2 = 1/2 P(0) + P(1) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 ✓

Set the sliders to θ = 90°, φ = 90° to see this point on the right-hand side of the equator (the +y axis).

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the half. The formula uses θ/2, not θ. |1⟩ is at θ = π (the south pole), because cos(π/2) = 0 and sin(π/2) = 1. Putting |1⟩ at θ = π/2 lands you on the equator instead.
  • Measuring θ like latitude. On a map, latitude starts at the equator. Here θ starts at the north pole: θ = 0 is |0⟩, θ = π/2 is the equator.
  • Thinking φ changes the odds. Spinning around the equator (changing φ) leaves P(0) = cos²(θ/2) unchanged. |+⟩ and |−⟩ both give 50/50 when measured in 0/1; only φ tells them apart.

Give θ and φ for |0⟩, |1⟩, |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2.

Check it in NumPy

import numpy as np
ket = lambda t, p: np.array([np.cos(t/2), np.exp(1j*p) * np.sin(t/2)])
for name, t, p in [("|0>", 0, 0), ("|1>", np.pi, 0), ("|+>", np.pi/2, 0), ("|->", np.pi/2, np.pi)]:
    print(name, np.round(ket(t, p), 4))
# |0> [1, 0]   |1> [0, 1]   |+> [0.7071, 0.7071]   |-> [0.7071, -0.7071]
