Linear algebra 1: vectors and inner products

A qubit's state is a list of complex numbers. Today you learn how to measure the length of such a list and how alike two lists are. Every quantum probability you meet later is built from these two operations.

Back to basics: Pythagoras and square roots

In a right-angled triangle, the two short sides a and b and the long side c (the hypotenuse) obey a² + b² = c². Read it aloud: "a squared plus b squared equals c squared." To get c itself, take the square root of both sides: c = √(a² + b²).

Tiny example: short sides 3 and 4.

3² = 3 × 3 = 9
4² = 4 × 4 = 16
9 + 16 = 25
c = √25 = 5 (because 5 × 5 = 25)

One more refresher from Day 1. For a complex number, "size squared" is a number times its own conjugate: z̄ · z = |z|². For z = i: ī · i = (−i)(i) = −i² = −(−1) = 1. So |i|² = 1, never −1. Lengths are never negative.

The idea

A vector is a list of numbers that you can picture as an arrow from the origin. Every vector is a mix (a linear combination) of a few fixed basis arrows, such as (1, 0) and (0, 1). Its length comes straight from Pythagoras. The inner product of two vectors is one number that says how much one points along the other. With complex entries you must conjugate the first vector, so that a vector's inner product with itself is always its length squared, a positive real number.

Pictures

Key definition

⟨u, v⟩ = ū₁v₁ + ū₂v₂ + … + ūₙvₙ
‖v‖ = √⟨v, v⟩   ·   v̂ = v ⁄ ‖v‖

Read it aloud: "The inner product of u and v equals u-one-bar times v-one, plus u-two-bar times v-two, and so on up to u-n-bar times v-n." Then: "The norm of v is the square root of the inner product of v with itself. v hat is v divided by its norm."

Worked example

Take u = (1, i) and v = (2, 3i). Find ⟨u, v⟩, then the length of u, then normalise u.

Part A: the inner product ⟨u, v⟩

Step 1. Conjugate u: ū = (1̄, ī) = (1, −i)
Step 2. First pair: ū₁ · v₁ = 1 · 2 = 2
Step 3. Second pair: ū₂ · v₂ = (−i) · (3i)
Multiply the plain numbers and the i's separately:
(−i) · (3i) = −3 · i · i = −3 · i²
i² = −1, so −3 · i² = −3 · (−1) = 3
Step 4. Add: ⟨u, v⟩ = 2 + 3 = 5

Part B: the length ‖u‖

⟨u, u⟩ = ū₁u₁ + ū₂u₂
ū₁u₁ = 1 · 1 = 1
ū₂u₂ = (−i) · (i) = −i² = −(−1) = 1
⟨u, u⟩ = 1 + 1 = 2
‖u‖ = √2 ≈ 1.414

Part C: normalise u

û = u ⁄ ‖u‖ = (1 ⁄ √2, i ⁄ √2)
Check: |1 ⁄ √2|² = 1 ⁄ 2
|i ⁄ √2|² = |i|² ⁄ 2 = 1 ⁄ 2
‖û‖² = 1 ⁄ 2 + 1 ⁄ 2 = 1, so ‖û‖ = 1 ✓
Check of Part A in NumPy: np.vdot([1, 1j], [2, 3j]) gives (5+0j). ✓

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to conjugate the first vector. Without it, u = (1, i) "dotted" with itself gives 1·1 + i·i = 1 + (−1) = 0, a non-zero vector with length 0. That is nonsense, and it is why the bar is there.
  • Squaring i instead of taking its modulus squared. The length of (1, i, 1) uses |i|² = 1, not i² = −1. Lengths add only positive pieces.
  • Using np.dot instead of np.vdot. np.dot does not conjugate: np.dot([1, 1j], [2, 3j]) gives −1, the wrong answer. np.vdot conjugates the first argument and gives 5.

Practice

Normalise v = (1, i, 1). Then compute the inner product of your normalised vector v̂ with e₁ = (1, 0, 0).

Show answer

Step 1: length of v

|1|² = 1
|i|² = ī · i = (−i)(i) = −i² = 1
|1|² = 1
⟨v, v⟩ = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3
‖v‖ = √3 ≈ 1.732

Step 2: divide every entry by √3

v̂ = (1 ⁄ √3, i ⁄ √3, 1 ⁄ √3)
1 ⁄ √3 ≈ 1 ⁄ 1.732 ≈ 0.577
v̂ ≈ (0.577, 0.577i, 0.577)
Check: 1⁄3 + 1⁄3 + 1⁄3 = 3⁄3 = 1 ✓ normalised

Step 3: inner product with e₁ = (1, 0, 0)

Conjugate v̂: (1 ⁄ √3, −i ⁄ √3, 1 ⁄ √3)
⟨v̂, e₁⟩ = (1 ⁄ √3)·1 + (−i ⁄ √3)·0 + (1 ⁄ √3)·0
= 1 ⁄ √3 + 0 + 0
= 1 ⁄ √3 ≈ 0.577 ✓
The zeros in e₁ simply pick out the first entry. Swapping the order, ⟨e₁, v̂⟩, gives the conjugate of this number; here the first entry is real, so it is also 1 ⁄ √3. Squared, (1 ⁄ √3)² = 1 ⁄ 3: on Day 5 you will see this is a probability.

Check it in NumPy

import numpy as np
v = np.array([1, 1j, 1])
v_hat = v / np.linalg.norm(v)              # divide by √3
print(np.linalg.norm(v), np.linalg.norm(v_hat))   # 1.732..., 1.0
print(np.vdot(v_hat, [1, 0, 0]))           # vdot conjugates the first: (0.577+0j)
